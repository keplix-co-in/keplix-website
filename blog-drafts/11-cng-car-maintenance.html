A CNG car is mostly a petrol car with a second fuel system bolted on, so most of its maintenance is the same: engine oil, filters, brakes, tyres. What changes is a short list of extra items. CNG burns hotter and drier than petrol, which is harder on spark plugs and valves, and the gas system itself has a filter, a pressure regulator (the reducer) and a high-pressure cylinder that all need periodic attention.

The one item that is not optional is the cylinder. In India, CNG cylinders must be hydro-tested at an approved centre every three years, and the date of the last test is stamped on the cylinder. Skip it and you are driving with an uncertified pressure vessel, and many filling stations will refuse to fill you.

Spark plugs and ignition wear faster

Petrol has some lubricating and cooling effect inside the combustion chamber. CNG has very little, and it needs a stronger spark to ignite reliably. The result is that spark plugs wear faster on a car that runs mostly on gas. Worn plugs show up as:

  • misfires or a shaky idle on CNG that is less noticeable on petrol;
  • jerking when you accelerate under load, for example on a flyover;
  • the car switching back to petrol on its own more often;
  • a drop in mileage on gas.

Check the owner's manual for the CNG plug interval, which is usually shorter than the petrol-only version of the same car. Use the plug type the manufacturer specifies. If plugs are fine but misfires continue, the ignition coils and plug leads are next on the list, since they work harder on CNG too.

The dry, hot combustion is also why some CNG engines need valve clearance checks at set intervals. Not every engine does, so follow the service schedule for your model rather than a general rule.

The CNG filter and the reducer

Gas from the cylinder is stored at high pressure. The reducer (also called the pressure regulator or vaporiser) brings it down to a pressure the injectors can use. Before it, a gas filter catches oil traces and fine particles that come in with the gas.

  • Filter: a clogged filter starves the engine. Symptoms are loss of power on CNG, hesitation and the car switching to petrol at higher loads. Filter replacement is a routine item on most CNG service schedules.
  • Reducer: it has internal diaphragms and seals that age. A worn reducer can cause uneven running, a smell of gas, or poor cold starting on CNG. Some reducers also collect an oily residue that a garage drains during service.

Both are jobs for a garage that actually works on CNG systems. Ask whether the CNG kit was checked at your service; it is easy for a general service to cover the petrol side only. Our guide on how to read a car service bill helps you see what was and was not done.

Cylinder hydro-testing every three years

Under India's Gas Cylinder Rules, 2016, administered by PESO (the Petroleum and Explosives Safety Organisation), CNG vehicle cylinders must be re-tested every three years. The test fills the cylinder with water at high pressure to check that it has not weakened, cracked or corroded.

What you need to know:

  • Find the date. The last test date is stamped on the cylinder, usually near the neck, and is often also on a compliance plate or in the fitment papers. The three years run from that date.
  • Use an approved centre. Testing must be done at a PESO-approved hydro-testing centre. There have been reports of outlets issuing certificates without doing a real test, so go to a recognised one, such as those listed by your city gas distribution company.
  • Keep the certificate. A passed cylinder gets a new date stamp and a certificate. Filling stations may ask to see it, and in Delhi, enforcement of the three-year test has been tightened after accidents at filling stations.
  • A failed cylinder is replaced, not repaired. Cylinders also have an overall service life set by their standard, after which they must be retired.

Leak checks

A small CNG leak is not always obvious. Natural gas has an odour added to it so leaks can be smelt, but in an open car park or a moving car you may not notice. Have the system checked:

  • at every regular service;
  • after any accident, even a minor rear-end knock, because the cylinder and its mounting sit in the boot;
  • immediately if you smell gas in or around the car, or if the CNG gauge drops faster than your driving explains.

A garage checks the joints and pipes with a leak-detection solution or an electronic detector, and inspects the cylinder mounting brackets, which can loosen on bad roads. If you smell gas while driving, switch to petrol, pull over somewhere open, switch off the engine and close the cylinder valve if you know where it is. Do not use a lighter or switch on anything to "check".

Start on petrol, and use the petrol now and then

Many CNG cars, especially factory-fitted ones, start on petrol and switch to CNG automatically once the engine warms up. That is by design: a cold engine runs better on petrol. Even on cars that allow direct starting on CNG, starting on petrol is generally gentler on the engine.

Keep some petrol in the tank, not just a few drops. The petrol pump and injectors need fuel moving through them to stay healthy, and petrol that sits for months goes stale. Running on petrol for a few kilometres every week or so keeps that side working, which matters when you run out of gas or a CNG station is closed.

Factory-fitted vs retrofit kits

Both can be fine, but they differ in ways that affect maintenance:

Factory-fittedRetrofit (aftermarket)
Engine tuningCalibrated for CNG by the maker, often with changes to engine partsDepends on the kit and the fitter
WarrantyCovered by the car maker's warranty, subject to its termsThe kit carries its own warranty. The car maker may decline claims on parts affected by the conversion
ServicingAt the brand's service centres, on the brand's scheduleBest done by the kit fitter or a garage that knows that kit
PaperworkCNG already on the registration certificateMust be approved type of kit, fitted by an authorised fitter, and the RC endorsed with the RTO

If you have a retrofit kit, check that CNG is recorded on your RC and that your insurer has been told. An unendorsed conversion can cause trouble at a claim, at a fitness check, and at resale.

A simple CNG maintenance checklist

  • Check the cylinder test date today and diary the next one.
  • Follow the CNG-specific service schedule, not the petrol one.
  • Spark plugs on the CNG interval; coils if misfires persist.
  • CNG filter and reducer checked at service.
  • Leak check at every service and after any knock to the rear.
  • Air filter clean. CNG engines are sensitive to airflow, and Delhi dust clogs filters quickly.
  • Keep petrol in the tank and use it occasionally.
