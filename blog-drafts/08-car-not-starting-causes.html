When a car will not start, the sound it makes when you turn the key or press the start button tells you most of what you need to know. A rapid clicking usually means a flat or weak battery. A slow, laboured crank points to the battery or its connections. If the engine cranks normally but will not fire, the problem is fuel, spark or the immobiliser, not the battery. Complete silence suggests a dead battery, a loose terminal, or a safety switch that is not being triggered.

Listen carefully, note what the dashboard does, and then work through the matching section below. Most of the checks are safe to do yourself. Where they are not, this guide says so.

Before anything else: the simple things

  • Gear position: automatics will usually only start in P or N. Move the lever to P firmly and try again.
  • Clutch pedal: most manual cars with push-button start, and many with a key, need the clutch pressed fully to the floor.
  • Brake pedal: push-button cars usually need the brake pressed as well.
  • Key fob battery: if a push-button car shows a "key not detected" message, hold the fob right against the start button or the spot your manual describes. Many cars can read a fob with a dead battery that way.
  • Steering lock: if the key will not turn, the steering lock may be engaged. Turn the wheel gently left and right while turning the key.
  • Fuel: it sounds obvious, but a faulty fuel gauge or a car parked on a slope can mislead you.

Symptom 1: rapid clicking, engine does not turn

A fast "click-click-click" with dim or flickering dashboard lights is the classic sign of a battery that has enough charge to work the starter solenoid but not enough to turn the engine.

Likely causes: a flat or failing battery, or corroded or loose battery terminals.

What you can check safely:

  • Look at the terminals. A white or greenish crust, or a clamp that moves when you wiggle it by hand, can stop current getting through. Do not touch both terminals at once, and keep metal tools away from them.
  • Think back: were the headlights, cabin light or a charger left on? Has the car been standing unused for a couple of weeks?

What usually fixes it: a jump-start, if you have cables, a donor car and know the correct connection order from your owner's manual. If you are not confident, ask for help instead; connecting cables the wrong way can damage the electrics. Once it starts, get the battery and charging system tested. A battery that needed a jump once will often need one again.

Symptom 2: slow, laboured cranking

The engine turns, but sluggishly, as if it is struggling, and may or may not catch.

Likely causes:

  • A weak or ageing battery, especially on the first start of the morning.
  • Poor connections at the battery or the earth strap.
  • A starter motor that is wearing out.
  • Engine oil that is far overdue for a change, or the wrong grade, which makes the engine harder to turn. Check when the oil was last changed.

A slow crank is the most useful warning you will get. The car may still start today, but it is telling you the battery is close to the end. Our guide to car battery life in India explains the other early signs and how a proper battery test works.

Symptom 3: cranks normally but will not fire

The starter spins the engine at a healthy speed, but the engine never catches. The battery is almost certainly fine. The engine is missing fuel, spark or permission to run.

Likely causes:

  • Immobiliser: if the car does not recognise the key, it will often crank but not start. Look for a flashing key or lock symbol on the dashboard. Try the spare key.
  • Fuel supply: an empty tank, a failed fuel pump, a blown fuel pump fuse or a clogged fuel filter. On many cars you can hear the fuel pump hum for a second or two when you switch the ignition on without cranking. If that sound is missing, the pump or its fuse is a suspect.
  • Ignition: on petrol cars, failed spark plugs or ignition coils.
  • Sensors: a failed crankshaft position sensor can stop the engine running entirely.
  • Flooded engine: repeated short start attempts on some petrol engines can leave too much fuel in the cylinders.
  • Water: if the car has been driven through deep water, stop. Do not keep cranking. Water inside the engine can cause severe damage. Get it towed and inspected.
  • CNG cars: if the car is set to start on CNG and the cylinder is low, try switching to petrol.

What not to do: do not crank continuously for long stretches. Crank for a few seconds at a time and wait between attempts. Long cranking overheats the starter and drains the battery, which turns one problem into two.

Symptom 4: nothing at all

You turn the key or press the button and nothing happens: no click, no crank, perhaps no dashboard lights.

No dashboard lights at all: the battery is completely flat, a terminal has come loose, or the main fuse has failed. Check the terminals first.

Dashboard lights come on, but no click or crank:

  • The gear, clutch or brake switch is not being triggered (see the simple checks above).
  • A starter relay or fuse has failed.
  • The starter motor itself has failed, or its wiring has come loose.

Fuses are a reasonable thing to check yourself if your owner's manual shows the fuse box layout and you have the correct spare. Always replace a fuse with one of the same rating. If a new fuse blows again straight away, there is a fault that needs a mechanic.

Warning lights worth noting

Before you call anyone, note any lights that stay on or flash on the dashboard. A flashing key symbol points to the immobiliser. A battery light that stayed on while you were driving the day before points to the alternator, not the battery. Our guide to dashboard warning lights explains the common ones. Telling the mechanic exactly what you saw saves time and guesswork.

When to stop and call for help

  • You smell fuel, or see fuel leaking under the car.
  • You see smoke, or smell burning from the engine bay.
  • The car was driven through deep water before it stopped.
  • A replaced fuse blows again.
  • A jump-start does not work, or works but the car dies again soon after.
  • You are not comfortable working near the battery.
