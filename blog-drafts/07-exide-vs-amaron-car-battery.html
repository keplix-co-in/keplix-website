For most cars, either brand will do the job. Exide and Amaron are both large, established battery makers in India, and both sell batteries built for Indian heat and traffic. The question that matters more is whether the battery you are offered matches your car's specification, and what its warranty actually promises once you read past the headline number.

So treat the choice in this order: first get the specs right, then compare the warranty terms on the exact models you are offered, then think about how easy it will be to claim that warranty where you live. The brand name comes last.

Step 1: match the specification, not the brand

Your car's owner's manual, or the label on the battery it came with, lists what the car needs. A replacement should match these closely:

  • Capacity (Ah, ampere-hours): how much charge the battery stores. Going far below the original rating leaves you short on cold mornings or with heavy electrical load. Going much higher is usually unnecessary and may not fit.
  • Cold cranking amps (CCA): how much current the battery can deliver in a short burst to turn the starter. Diesel engines generally need more cranking power than petrol engines of similar size. Do not go below what the manufacturer specifies.
  • Size and terminal layout: the battery has to fit the tray and hold-down clamp, and the positive and negative terminals must sit on the correct sides. A battery with reversed terminals may not reach the cables at all, or may force them into an unsafe stretch.
  • Battery type for start-stop cars: cars with auto start-stop are often designed for a specific battery type, such as EFB or AGM. Fitting an ordinary flooded battery in its place can shorten its life and may stop the start-stop system working properly. Check the manual.

Both brands publish fitment lists by car model, and any good battery shop will look up your car rather than guess. If the shop suggests a battery with a noticeably different Ah or CCA figure from the original, ask why.

Step 2: understand how the warranty is structured

Battery warranties in India are usually advertised as a single number of months, but that number often hides two different kinds of cover. Read the warranty card for the exact model before you pay.

Free replacement period

During the first part of the warranty, if the battery is found to be defective, it is replaced at no charge. This is the part of the warranty that actually protects you.

Pro-rata period

Some batteries carry a second, pro-rata period after the free replacement period ends. If the battery fails during this time, you do not get a free replacement. Instead you get a discount on a new battery, and the discount shrinks the closer you are to the end of the warranty. A battery advertised with a long total warranty may have a shorter free replacement window inside it.

Warranty terms differ between product ranges within the same brand and change over time, so compare the actual models on offer rather than relying on what a friend got a few years ago.

What can void a battery warranty

Terms vary, but claims are commonly refused for:

  • physical damage, a cracked case or tampering with the battery;
  • a missing or unfilled warranty card, or a missing purchase invoice;
  • the battery being used in a different vehicle from the one registered;
  • damage caused by an electrical fault in the car, such as an overcharging alternator.

Keep the invoice and the warranty card together, ideally with a photo of both on your phone. Make sure the date of sale is filled in. Some brands also let you register the warranty online; if yours does, do it the same day.

Step 3: think about after-sales service where you are

A warranty is only as good as your ability to claim it. Both brands have wide dealer networks, but what matters is the dealer or service point near your home or office. Before buying, ask:

  • Where would I take the battery if it fails, and do they test it on the spot?
  • Will I get a temporary battery while the claim is processed?
  • Is there a home pick-up or roadside battery service in my area?

The answers can differ from one city, and even one neighbourhood, to the next. A brand with a responsive service point ten minutes away is a better choice for you than one whose nearest claim centre is across town.

When to lean one way or the other

Neither brand is objectively better for every car. These are the practical tie-breakers:

  • Choose the one your car's maker fitted, if you were happy with it. Many cars leave the factory with one of these two brands. If the original battery lasted well, a like-for-like replacement is the lowest-risk choice.
  • Choose the longer free replacement period when the specs of the two options match. That is the part of the warranty you are most likely to use.
  • Choose the one with the closer, better-reviewed service point if the warranties are similar.
  • Choose the correct type first for start-stop cars. If only one brand has the right EFB or AGM battery for your car in stock, that settles it.
  • Do not choose on price alone. A cheaper battery with a shorter free replacement window, or a lower CCA rating, can cost more over its life.

Buying checks that matter more than the brand

  1. Check the manufacturing date. A battery slowly loses charge sitting on a shelf. Ask for one made recently; the date is usually coded on the case or the label, and the shop can decode it for you.
  2. Ask for a voltage check before fitting. A fresh battery should be fully charged when it goes in.
  3. Get the charging system tested. If the old battery died young, the alternator or a parasitic drain may be the cause. A new battery will not fix that, and it may fail the same way. Our guide to car battery life and the signs of a dying battery explains what a proper test involves.
  4. Return the old battery. Most shops take it back as part of the exchange. Old lead-acid batteries should go to an authorised recycler, not the scrap heap.
  5. Check the terminals and clamp after fitting. Terminals should be tight and clean, and the battery should not move in its tray.

A note on newer cars

On some newer cars, the battery management system needs to be told that a new battery has been fitted, so it charges it correctly. This is done with a diagnostic tool. Not every car needs it, but if yours does, skipping it can shorten the new battery's life. Ask the person fitting the battery whether your car requires it.
