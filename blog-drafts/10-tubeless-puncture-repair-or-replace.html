A puncture in the main tread of a tubeless tyre, from a nail or a screw, can usually be repaired safely, as long as the hole is small and the tyre was not driven far while flat. A puncture in the sidewall or the shoulder, a large cut, or a tyre that has been run flat usually means replacement. The best repair is done from inside the tyre, with the tyre taken off the rim, not just a plug pushed in from outside.

The rest of this guide explains the difference between the repair types, where on the tyre a repair is and is not acceptable, and when you should stop repairing and buy a new tyre.

First: do not drive on it flat

One advantage of tubeless tyres is that they often lose air slowly around a nail, so you may have time to reach a puncture shop. But driving any distance on a tyre that is very low or flat crushes the sidewall from inside. The damage is often invisible from outside, and it can make an otherwise repairable tyre unsafe. If the tyre is visibly flat, change to the spare or call for help.

If the nail is still in the tyre, leave it there until you reach the shop. Pulling it out usually makes the leak much faster.

Plug, patch or mushroom patch

Plug (string repair, from outside)

This is the quick roadside fix most puncture shops offer. A sticky rubber string is pushed into the hole from outside with a tool, without taking the tyre off the wheel.

  • Good for: getting you moving again quickly.
  • Limitation: nobody looks inside the tyre, so internal damage from running low goes unnoticed. The plug also seals the hole without sealing the inner liner around it properly.

Many tyre makers and industry bodies consider a plug on its own a temporary repair. Treat it as a way to get home, and have the tyre inspected and properly repaired soon.

Patch (from inside)

The tyre is taken off the rim. The inside is inspected, the area around the hole is buffed and a patch is bonded to the inner liner.

  • Good for: sealing the inner liner, and the mechanic can see whether there is hidden damage.
  • Limitation: a patch alone does not fill the hole through the tread, so moisture can get into the tyre's structure over time.

Mushroom patch, or plug-patch combination

This combines both: a stem fills the hole through the tread and a patch seals the inside, in one piece, fitted from inside with the tyre off the rim. It is widely regarded as the most complete repair for a passenger car tyre.

Not every roadside puncture shop does inside repairs. Ask before they start. After any repair that involves removing the tyre, the wheel should be balanced again.

The sidewall and shoulder rule

Where the puncture is matters as much as how big it is.

  • Tread area (repairable): the central part of the tread, the part that grips the road. Most small punctures here can be repaired.
  • Shoulder (usually not repairable): the curved edge where the tread meets the sidewall. This area flexes a lot, and repairs there tend not to hold. Most tyre makers advise against repairing it.
  • Sidewall (not repairable): the side of the tyre. It flexes constantly and carries much of the load. A cut, bulge or puncture here means the tyre should be replaced.

Size matters too. Industry guidance commonly limits repairs to small punctures, about the size of an ordinary nail or screw. A large cut or a tear in the tread is a replacement, not a repair. Check your tyre maker's guidance, since limits differ between makers.

How many repairs are too many?

There is no single number that applies to every tyre. Tyre makers differ, and the answer depends on where the repairs are. General guidance:

  • Repairs should not overlap. Two repairs very close together weaken the same area. If a new puncture is right next to an old repair, replace the tyre.
  • Several repairs on one tyre is a reason to ask whether it is worth keeping, especially if it is also worn.
  • Worn tyres are not worth repairing. If the tread is close to the wear indicators, put the money towards a new tyre. Our tyre care guide explains how to check tread depth.
  • Replace in pairs where needed. If one tyre is replaced and the others are much more worn, ask whether the pair on that axle should be replaced together for even grip.

Run-flat tyres: be careful

Some cars come with run-flat tyres, which have reinforced sidewalls that let you drive a limited distance at reduced speed after a puncture. The exact distance and speed are set by the tyre or car maker, so check your owner's manual.

Repair is a different question. Many tyre makers say a run-flat that has been driven while deflated should not be repaired, because the internal damage cannot be judged reliably. Some do allow repair in specific conditions. If your car has run-flats, ask a tyre specialist who knows that make, rather than a roadside shop, and follow the tyre maker's guidance.

Cars with run-flats often have no spare tyre, so know this before a long drive.

Puncture sealant gel

Some cars come with a tyre repair kit (a small compressor and a bottle of sealant) instead of a spare, and aftermarket sealant gels are widely sold.

  • What it is good for: sealing a small tread puncture well enough to get you to a tyre shop.
  • What it will not do: seal a sidewall puncture, a large cut, or a tyre that has come off the rim.
  • Tell the tyre shop you used it. Sealant can make an inside repair messier, and some types can affect tyre pressure sensors. Follow the product's instructions on speed and distance after use.
  • Pre-filled gel as prevention: some owners fill their tyres with sealant in advance. It can reduce slow leaks, but it does not replace a proper repair, and some tyre shops dislike working on gel-filled tyres. Ask your tyre shop before using it.

Quick decision guide

  • Small nail or screw in the tread, not driven flat: repair, ideally with a mushroom patch from inside.
  • Puncture in the shoulder or sidewall: replace.
  • Cut, bulge or tear: replace.
  • Driven any distance while flat: have it inspected inside before deciding.
  • New puncture next to an old repair: replace.
  • Tread near the wear indicators: replace.
