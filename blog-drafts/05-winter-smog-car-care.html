From November to February, much of North India deals with a combination that is hard on cars and drivers: dense morning fog, heavy smog, cold starts, and pollution-control rules that can change at short notice. None of it calls for anything dramatic, but a few checks and habits make a real difference.

Visibility comes first

Most winter accidents come down to not seeing, or not being seen.

  • Headlights: check that both work on low and high beam and that the lenses are clear. Yellowed, hazy headlight lenses scatter light badly in fog. Restoring them is inexpensive.
  • Fog lamps: if your car has them, confirm they work and are aimed low. Use them only in genuine fog, not as a style feature, because they dazzle oncoming drivers in clear air.
  • Use low beam in fog, not high beam. High beam reflects off the fog and makes visibility worse.
  • Wipers and washer fluid: smog leaves a greasy film on the windscreen. Fresh wiper blades and a proper washer fluid, not plain water, clear it far better.
  • Demisting: the AC is the fastest way to clear a fogged-up windscreen, even in winter, because it dries the air. Use the AC with the windscreen vent setting.
  • Rear fog lamp: if your car has one, use it only in thick fog. It is bright enough to dazzle the driver behind in clear air.

Your cabin filter matters more in smog season

The cabin air filter is the only thing between outside air and your lungs while you drive. Standard filters catch dust. Activated-carbon cabin filters also reduce some odours and gases. In heavy smog:

  • Replace an old cabin filter before the season starts. A clogged one cuts airflow and filters poorly. Aftermarket filters sold as "PM2.5" or "HEPA-type" for your model are widely available. Check that the size matches exactly, because a filter that leaves gaps lets air bypass it.
  • Use recirculation mode in heavy traffic to avoid pulling in exhaust from the vehicle ahead, but switch to fresh air periodically so the windows do not fog and the cabin air does not go stale.
  • Be wary of claims that a cabin filter makes the air "clean". It helps, but it is not a medical air purifier.

Cold starts

  • Battery: a cold morning is when a weak battery finally fails. If the engine has been cranking slowly, get the battery tested before the coldest weeks. Our battery guide lists the early signs.
  • No need to idle for long. Modern engines need only a short warm-up. Start, wait a few seconds for the oil to circulate, and drive gently for the first few kilometres. Long idling wastes fuel and adds pollution.
  • Engine oil: make sure the grade in the engine matches your manual. The first number, such as the 5W in 5W-30, describes cold-flow performance.
  • Coolant: despite the name, it matters in winter too, both for protecting against corrosion and, in hill areas, against freezing. Check the level and that it is the correct type.

Pollution certificates and restrictions

When air quality worsens, authorities in some cities bring in stricter enforcement of pollution rules and can restrict certain categories of vehicles. In Delhi-NCR this happens through the staged Graded Response Action Plan (GRAP), whose later stages can bar some older petrol and diesel cars from the roads. Rules vary by city and change with conditions, so check current local advisories before you drive. Things within your control:

  • Keep your PUC certificate valid. It is a legal requirement, and enforcement checks become more frequent in smog season.
  • Note your vehicle's fuel type and emission standard (for example BS-IV or BS-VI). Restrictions are often defined by these.
  • A well-maintained engine emits less. Clogged air filters, old spark plugs and overdue oil changes all increase emissions and fuel use.

Driving habits for fog

  • Slow down and leave a much bigger gap than usual. In fog, you see hazards late.
  • Use the edge markings or the road edge as a guide, not the tail lights ahead. Following tail lights closely is how chain collisions start.
  • Avoid overtaking in fog.
  • Do not drive with hazard lights on. It is a common habit in fog, but it means your indicators stop working, so nobody can tell when you are turning or changing lanes. Use dipped headlights and fog lamps instead. Keep hazards for when you are stopped.
  • If visibility becomes dangerously low, pull completely off the road at a safe spot with hazard lights on, rather than stopping in the lane.
  • Allow extra time. Rushing in fog is the real risk.
