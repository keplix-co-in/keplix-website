Every festive season, the highways out of every big city fill up at once. A breakdown on an ordinary weekday is an inconvenience. A breakdown on a packed highway at night, 300 km from home, with family in the car, is a different problem. Most of those breakdowns come from a short list of things that could have been spotted in the driveway.

One week before: book anything that needs a garage

Garages are busiest in the days just before a festival, and rushed work is not what you want before a long drive. If any of the following apply, get them looked at a week or more ahead:

  • Your service is due, or nearly due, by date or by kilometres.
  • A warning light has been on "for a while".
  • The brakes squeal, grind, or the pedal feels softer than it used to.
  • The car pulls to one side, or the steering wheel vibrates at highway speed.
  • The battery is more than three years old and the engine has been cranking slowly in the mornings.

The 30-minute driveway check

Do this with the engine cold, on level ground, a day or two before you leave.

Tyres (10 minutes)

  • Pressure: check all four plus the spare, cold, using the figures on the sticker inside the driver's door. Many cars list a higher pressure for a full load. Use it if the car will be full of people and luggage.
  • Tread: look for the small wear bars in the grooves, which sit at about 1.6 mm. If the tread is level with them, the tyre is due for replacement, and it is wise to replace sooner if you will be driving long highway stretches or in rain.
  • Sidewalls: look for bulges, cuts or cracks. A bulge means internal damage. Do not take that tyre on a long trip.
  • The spare: check its pressure too. A thin "space-saver" spare usually needs a much higher pressure than the main tyres. The figure is printed on its sidewall or on the door sticker. Also confirm the jack and wheel spanner are in the car and that you know where the locking wheel nut key is, if your car has one.

Fluids (5 minutes)

  • Engine oil: pull the dipstick, wipe, reinsert, check. It should sit between the two marks.
  • Coolant: check the level in the translucent reservoir against the MIN/MAX marks. Never open the radiator cap on a hot engine.
  • Brake fluid: the level should be near MAX. A noticeably low level can mean worn pads or a leak. Get it checked before the trip.
  • Washer fluid: top it up. Highway insects and dust make a clean windscreen a safety item.

Lights and wipers (5 minutes)

Walk round the car with someone pressing the controls: headlights low and high beam, indicators, brake lights, reverse lights and hazards. Check that the wipers clear the glass without streaking. Many festival drives end after dark.

Under the car and around it (5 minutes)

  • Look at the ground where the car has been parked overnight. Fresh drips are worth identifying: dark brown is usually oil, green or pink is often coolant, and clear, odourless water dripping near the front, below the dashboard area, is normally just AC condensation.
  • Check that nothing is hanging loose underneath.

Documents and kit (5 minutes)

  • Registration certificate, valid insurance, PUC certificate and your licence, physical or in DigiLocker.
  • A working FASTag with enough balance for the whole round trip.
  • Your insurer's roadside assistance number saved in your phone, if your policy includes it.
  • A phone charger, a torch, drinking water, and a basic first-aid kit.

On the road

  • Watch the temperature gauge in slow festival traffic. If it climbs towards hot, switch off the AC, turn the heater to full to pull heat away from the engine, and pull over safely if it keeps rising.
  • Do not ignore a new warning light because you are "nearly there". A red oil pressure or temperature light means stop as soon as it is safe.
  • Take a break every two hours. Fatigue is a major cause of highway crashes, and festival trips often mean an early start after a late night of packing.
  • Plan for toll queues. Plazas back up badly on the peak travel days. Keep an eye on the temperature gauge while crawling through them, and avoid riding the clutch in a manual.
  • Recheck tyre pressures at a fuel stop on the return leg if the car has been loaded with gifts and sweets.

During the festival itself

  • Park away from where firecrackers are being lit. Sparks and hot debris can scorch paint, melt plastic trim and burn holes in car covers, which are often synthetic and catch easily. A covered or enclosed spot is safest on Diwali night.
  • Close the windows and sunroof fully if the car is parked outdoors overnight.
  • Wash off ash and residue gently the next day with plenty of water before wiping. Dry-wiping the gritty residue scratches the paint.
  • Keep a basic fire extinguisher in the car if you can. It is useful all year, and especially on Diwali night.

When you get back

Long trips are a good moment to note anything that felt different: a new noise, a vibration at a particular speed, brakes that faded on a long descent. Write it down while you remember, and mention it at your next service rather than waiting for it to get worse.
