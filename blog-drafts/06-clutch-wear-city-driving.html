In a manual car, the clutch plate is a wear item, like brake pads. How long it lasts depends far more on how and where the car is driven than on the car itself. The same clutch might last anywhere from around 40,000 km to well over 1,00,000 km. Dense city traffic and hilly routes push it towards the low end, and a few habits make a large difference.

How a clutch wears

The clutch connects the engine to the gearbox through the clutch plate (a friction disc) pressed between the flywheel and the pressure plate. Every time the clutch slips, while you are partly pressing the pedal, that friction material wears a little. Full engagement and full disengagement cause almost no wear. The slipping in between is what uses it up.

That is why city traffic is so hard on clutches. Crawling, starting on slopes and creeping in queues all involve long periods of slipping.

Signs of a worn clutch

  • Slipping: the engine revs rise but the car does not speed up to match, especially when accelerating hard in a higher gear or climbing a flyover. This is the classic sign of a worn-out clutch plate.
  • A high biting point: the clutch engages only near the very top of the pedal travel. This usually rises gradually as the disc wears.
  • A burning smell after a slope start or heavy traffic. It is sometimes normal after hard use, but if it happens often the clutch is slipping a lot.
  • Judder: the car shudders as you move off from a stop.
  • Difficulty selecting gears, especially first and reverse, can point to a clutch that is not fully disengaging. It can also be a hydraulic or cable problem rather than wear.
  • New noises when pressing or releasing the pedal can indicate a worn release bearing.

The habits that wear clutches out

  • Riding the clutch: resting your foot on the pedal while driving. Even light pressure can partly disengage it and cause wear.
  • Holding the car on a slope with the clutch instead of the brake or handbrake. It is one of the fastest ways to wear out a clutch.
  • Creeping in traffic on the clutch instead of letting the car roll in first gear with the clutch fully up, or simply waiting and then moving.
  • Sitting at signals in gear with the pedal pressed down for long periods. It wears the release bearing. Shift to neutral and release the pedal at long stops.
  • High-rev starts: moving off with far more revs than needed.

The habits that help

  • Take your foot completely off the pedal once you are moving. Use the dead pedal (the footrest on the left) if your car has one.
  • Use the handbrake for slope starts and release it as the clutch bites.
  • In slow-moving traffic, leave a gap and let the car move in first gear with the clutch fully released, rather than slipping it continuously.
  • Select neutral at long stops.
  • Move off with modest revs and engage the clutch smoothly but promptly.

AMT cars have a clutch too

An AMT (automated manual) has no clutch pedal, but inside it is a normal clutch operated by an actuator, and it wears the same way. The habit that hurts it most is holding the car still on a slope or in a creeping queue with the accelerator, which makes the actuator slip the clutch. Hold the car on the brake instead, and use hill-hold or the handbrake on steep slopes. If your AMT starts to judder when moving off, or the gear changes feel slower and harsher than before, get the clutch and actuator checked. Some symptoms can be fixed by a recalibration at the garage rather than new parts.

What a replacement involves

Replacing a clutch usually means removing the gearbox, which makes it a labour-heavy job. Because of that, it is normal practice to replace the components together as a kit, often called a "clutch set": the clutch plate, the pressure plate and the release bearing. Paying the labour twice to replace them separately makes little sense.

Ask the garage:

  • Is it a full kit? And which brand: original equipment or aftermarket?
  • What is the condition of the flywheel? It may need resurfacing, or replacing if it is a dual-mass type that has worn. This can significantly change the cost, so you want to know before work starts.
  • Can I see the old parts? A worn disc is easy to recognise once you have seen one.
  • Is a hydraulic or cable issue ruled out? Some clutch symptoms come from the clutch master or slave cylinder, or the cable, and those are cheaper fixes.
