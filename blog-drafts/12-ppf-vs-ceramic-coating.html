Paint protection film (PPF) and ceramic coating are often sold as if they do the same job. They do not. PPF is a thick, clear plastic film that physically absorbs stone chips, light scratches and scuffs. Ceramic coating is a thin, hard liquid layer that bonds to the paint and makes it glossier, water-repellent and easier to clean, but it does very little against impacts.

So the short answer: if you worry about chips and scratches, PPF is the one that actually protects. If you mostly want shine and easier washing, ceramic coating is the lighter option. Many owners do both, with a coating on top of the film.

What PPF actually does

PPF is a transparent film, usually thermoplastic polyurethane, applied over the painted panels. It is much thicker than any coating, and that thickness is the point. When a stone flicks up from the truck in front, the film takes the hit instead of the paint.

  • Impact protection: stone chips, gravel rash on the bonnet and bumper, door-edge dings in parking.
  • Scratch resistance: light swirl marks and fine scratches. Many films have a "self-healing" top layer that lets light scratches fade with heat, such as sunlight or warm water. Deep scratches through the film do not heal.
  • Chemical barrier: it keeps bird droppings, tree sap and tar off the paint itself.

Films come in gloss and matte finishes, and can cover the whole car or just high-impact areas: front bumper, bonnet, mirrors, door edges and the area behind the wheels.

The weak points are installation and quality. A poorly fitted film can lift at the edges, trap dust under it, or show visible joins. Cheap films can yellow or crack over time. When film is removed, poor-quality adhesive or careless removal can pull at repainted or weak paint.

What ceramic coating actually does

A ceramic coating is a liquid, typically silica-based, that is applied to clean, polished paint and cures into a very thin hard layer. What it gives you:

  • Gloss: the paint looks deeper and wetter, especially on dark colours.
  • Hydrophobic surface: water beads and runs off, taking some dirt with it.
  • Easier washing: dust and grime do not stick as firmly, so washes are quicker and need less scrubbing.
  • Some chemical and UV resistance: it buys you a little more time before bird droppings or sap etch the paint.

What it does not do is stop stone chips or scratches in any meaningful way. Claims of "9H hardness" refer to a pencil hardness test of the coating itself, and a coating that thin cannot absorb a stone impact. It can still get swirl marks if the car is washed badly.

Because the coating locks in whatever is underneath, the paint correction (polishing) before it matters as much as the coating. Coating over swirled paint preserves the swirls.

Questions to ask the installer

  • Which exact product? Ask for the brand and product name, not just "premium film" or "9H coating", so you can read its warranty terms yourself.
  • What prep is included? For a coating, ask whether paint correction is part of the job or extra. For PPF, ask how the panels are cleaned and decontaminated before fitting.
  • Pre-cut or hand-cut film? Pre-cut kits avoid blades on your paint; hand-cut fitting can wrap edges better but needs a skilled installer. Ask how they avoid cutting on the car.
  • How long will the car be with them? Both jobs need time to cure or settle, and rushing them shows later.
  • What does aftercare involve? Inspections, top-up coats and what voids the warranty.

How long each lasts

Both depend heavily on product quality, installation, how much the car is driven, and how it is washed. As a rough guide only:

  • PPF: good-quality films are commonly sold with multi-year warranties, often in the range of five years or more. Real life can be shorter on a car parked outdoors in strong sun.
  • Ceramic coating: anywhere from around one year to several years, depending on the product and how it is looked after. Claims of very long life usually assume regular maintenance washes and periodic top-ups.

Read what the warranty actually covers. Many cover yellowing, cracking or peeling of the product, not damage from accidents, and some require periodic inspections at the installer to stay valid. Keep the invoice and warranty card.

Wash care for both

  • Wait before the first wash. Coatings need time to cure and film needs time for the adhesive to settle. Ask your installer how long; it is usually several days.
  • Use a pH-neutral car shampoo. Strong detergents and harsh "bike wash" chemicals degrade coatings faster.
  • Rinse before you touch. Delhi dust is abrasive. Wiping a dusty car with a dry cloth scratches paint, coating and film alike.
  • Two buckets and a clean microfibre mitt instead of a single bucket and an old cloth.
  • Pressure washers: keep a sensible distance and do not aim directly at PPF edges, which can lift.
  • Avoid automatic brush washes if you can; the brushes are hard on any finish.
  • Remove bird droppings quickly, with a soaked microfibre, not by scraping.

Which one for Delhi NCR conditions?

Think about what actually damages your car:

  • Highway driving, expressways, construction zones: stone chips are the main risk. PPF on at least the front end (bumper, bonnet, mirrors) is worth considering.
  • Tight parking, busy markets, society basements: door dings and scuffs. PPF on door edges, bumpers and handle cups helps.
  • Parked outdoors under trees: bird droppings and sap. Either helps, but neither replaces cleaning them off quickly. In summer heat, droppings can etch unprotected paint fast.
  • Constant dust: a ceramic coating makes the frequent washes easier and gentler, which reduces wash scratches over time.
  • Monsoon: a hydrophobic surface sheds water and muddy splashes more easily. Neither product protects the underbody, which is where monsoon damage usually happens; see monsoon car care that actually matters.

A sensible middle ground for many city cars is partial PPF on the front and high-contact areas, with a ceramic coating over the whole car. If your car already has chips or scratches, get those repaired first. Film and coating both lock in whatever is underneath. A garage can handle that through denting and painting before protection goes on.
