Car AC systems use one of two refrigerants. Most cars sold in India still use R134a. A growing number, mainly newer and imported models, use R1234yf, a newer gas with a far lower climate impact. The quickest way to find out which your car has is the label under the bonnet, usually on the bonnet's underside, the radiator support or near the AC service ports.

The two are not interchangeable. A car must be refilled with the exact gas and quantity it was designed for, using equipment made for that gas. And if the gas is low, there is a leak somewhere: a top-up without a leak check is money that leaks away.

Which cars use which gas

R134a has been the standard car AC refrigerant for decades, and most cars built for the Indian market still use it. R1234yf is now standard in many cars built for Europe, the US and Japan, so you are more likely to find it on imported models and on cars from brands that build to a global specification. There is no simple rule by brand or year, so check your own car rather than assuming.

How to read the under-bonnet label

The AC label is a small sticker that typically shows:

  • Refrigerant type: "R134a", "HFC-134a", "R1234yf" or "HFO-1234yf".
  • Charge quantity: the exact amount of gas the system takes, in grams.
  • Compressor oil type, often a PAG grade.
  • Often a warning symbol. On R1234yf systems this usually includes a flammability symbol.

The service ports are another clue: R1234yf systems use differently sized port fittings, which is deliberate, to stop the wrong gas or equipment being connected. If the sticker is missing, your owner's manual or the brand's service centre can confirm it.

Why they cannot be mixed

  • Different properties. The two gases run at slightly different pressures and the system, including the expansion valve and charge amount, is designed for one. A mix behaves like neither, and cooling suffers.
  • Different oils. Each system uses a compressor oil matched to its gas. The wrong oil can damage the compressor, which is one of the most expensive AC parts.
  • Contaminated gas cannot be recovered properly. A mixed system has to be fully evacuated, and the contaminated gas disposed of, before it can be charged correctly.
  • Safety and equipment. R1234yf is mildly flammable, so it is handled differently (see below).

Be wary of "universal" top-up cans and of anyone offering to "convert" an R1234yf car to R134a to save money. Converting a system away from its designed gas is not something manufacturers support, and it can cause problems with the warranty and the compressor.

Leak first, then top-up

Refrigerant runs in a sealed loop and does not get used up. If the system is low, it has leaked. A proper AC gas job looks like this:

  1. Check pressures and confirm low gas is actually the problem, not a blocked cabin filter or a dirty condenser.
  2. Find the leak with UV dye, an electronic detector, or a pressure test. Common spots are pipe O-rings, the condenser, and the compressor shaft seal.
  3. Fix the leak.
  4. Vacuum the system to remove air and moisture. Moisture inside an AC system causes corrosion and poor cooling.
  5. Charge by weight to the exact amount on the label, with the correct oil.

A system topped up without this process often works for a few weeks and then fades again. Our post on why a car AC stops cooling covers the other causes to rule out first.

R1234yf is mildly flammable, so it needs the right equipment

R1234yf is classed as a mildly flammable refrigerant (safety class A2L). In a car's sealed system this is managed by design, but it does mean workshops need to handle it properly:

  • Dedicated recovery and recharge machines built for R1234yf, with its specific fittings.
  • Leak detectors suitable for that gas.
  • No naked flames or sparks near the work, and a well-ventilated area.
  • Correct disposal of recovered gas rather than venting it.

R1234yf also tends to cost more than R134a and is less widely stocked, so not every roadside AC shop can service it. Before booking, ask the garage whether they have R1234yf equipment if your label says you need it.

Signs your AC gas is low

  • Air from the vents is cool but not cold, even on maximum.
  • Cooling fades slowly over weeks rather than stopping suddenly.
  • The compressor clicks on and off more often than usual (short cycling).
  • Hissing near the dashboard just after you switch the AC off can be normal pressure equalising, but a constant hiss or oily patches on AC pipes and joints suggest a leak.
  • Frost or ice on AC pipes in the engine bay.

None of these prove low gas on their own. Weak airflow points to a blower or cabin filter problem, and good cooling while moving but not at signals usually points to the condenser fan. A pressure check settles it.

Keeping the AC healthy between services

  • Run the AC regularly, even in winter. The refrigerant carries oil around the system, and that oil keeps the compressor seals supple. A few minutes every week or two helps, and the AC also demists the windscreen in Delhi's foggy months.
  • Change the cabin filter on schedule, sooner if you drive in heavy dust.
  • Have the condenser cleaned before summer if it is visibly clogged with dirt or insects.
  • Do not ignore new noises when the AC switches on. A failing compressor can spread debris through the whole system, turning a single repair into several.

Where regulation is heading

R134a is a hydrofluorocarbon (HFC) with a high global warming potential. India has ratified the Kigali Amendment to the Montreal Protocol, which commits it to phasing down HFCs. India's schedule is a later one among developing countries: a freeze followed by stepped reductions starting in 2032. That phase-down applies to overall HFC consumption, not a ban on any one car, so existing R134a cars will continue to be serviced with R134a. Over time, more new cars sold in India are likely to move to lower-impact refrigerants, but no switch date for cars has been announced that we can point you to.

Before your next AC service

  • Check the label for your gas type and charge quantity.
  • Ask for a leak check before agreeing to a top-up.
  • Ask that the gas be charged by weight, and that the amount be written on the bill.
  • For R1234yf cars, confirm the garage has the right equipment.
