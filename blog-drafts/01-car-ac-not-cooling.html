When a car's AC stops cooling, the first suggestion is almost always a gas top-up. Sometimes that is right. Often it is not, and a top-up that leaks out again in three weeks is money spent twice. Weak cooling has a handful of common causes, and they look different once you know what to watch for.

First, describe the symptom precisely

A good mechanic will ask you questions before touching the car. Have the answers ready, because they narrow the problem down faster than any gauge:

  • Is the air weak, or warm? Strong airflow that is not cold points to the refrigerant side. Cold air that barely comes out of the vents points to the blower, the cabin filter or a blockage.
  • Does it cool while driving but not at a signal? That classic pattern usually means the condenser is not getting enough airflow at low speed: a weak radiator fan, or a condenser clogged with dust and dead insects.
  • Did it fade slowly over weeks, or stop suddenly? A slow fade suggests a small leak. A sudden stop suggests an electrical fault, a failed compressor clutch or a blown fuse.
  • Any new noise when the AC switches on? A grinding or rattling sound from the engine bay when you press the AC button needs attention quickly. It can be the compressor.

The common causes, roughly from cheapest to most expensive

1. A blocked cabin filter

The cabin filter cleans the air entering the cabin. In dusty Indian cities it can clog in a few months, and a clogged filter chokes airflow so badly that a healthy AC feels weak. It is cheap, takes minutes to replace, and is the first thing to check. If your service history does not mention it, ask.

2. A dirty condenser

The condenser sits in front of the radiator and sheds the heat the AC removes from the cabin. When it is caked with dirt, the system cannot get rid of that heat and cooling suffers, especially in traffic. Cleaning it is a modest job and often makes a surprising difference before summer.

3. Low refrigerant, which means a leak

Refrigerant does not get "used up". If the level is low, it has leaked out somewhere. A top-up alone treats the symptom. A proper job finds the leak first, usually with UV dye or an electronic detector, then fixes it, then vacuums the system to remove air and moisture, and only then recharges it by weight to the amount printed on the sticker under the bonnet.

Common leak points are O-rings at the pipe joints, the condenser (stones thrown up from the road can puncture it), and the compressor shaft seal.

4. Electrical faults

Relays, fuses, pressure switches and the compressor clutch coil can all stop the compressor engaging. These are often inexpensive parts, but finding them takes diagnosis rather than guesswork.

5. The compressor

The compressor is the heart of the system and the most expensive part to replace. Be cautious if a compressor replacement is the very first suggestion without any pressure readings or electrical checks. Compressors do fail, but they are also blamed for problems that turn out to be a relay.

What a proper diagnosis looks like

You do not need to understand the gauges, but you should see the process:

  1. A vent temperature reading with the AC on full. With recirculation on and the fan on a medium setting, a healthy system usually brings the centre vent down to somewhere around 5–12°C within a few minutes, even on a hot day. Air in the high teens or warmer points to a real problem.
  2. Pressure readings on the high and low sides with a gauge set. These tell the mechanic whether the problem is refrigerant, airflow or the compressor.
  3. A leak check if the refrigerant is low.
  4. A clear explanation of what was found, before any work starts.

Questions worth asking before you approve the work

  • "Where is the leak?" If they are recommending a recharge and cannot answer, the gas will leak out again.
  • "How much refrigerant will you put in, and how do you measure it?" The right answer is the weight on the specification sticker, not "until it feels cold".
  • "Is the cabin filter included, and can I see the old one?"
  • "If the compressor needs replacing, why? What readings show that?"
  • "Will you flush the system?" After a compressor failure, debris can spread through the pipes, and a new compressor fitted without a flush may fail early.

Small habits that keep the AC healthy

  • Run the AC for a few minutes every week, even in winter. It keeps the seals lubricated, and dry seals are a common source of slow leaks.
  • On very hot days, open the windows for the first minute to dump the hot air, then close them and switch to recirculation.
  • Replace the cabin filter at the interval in your manual, or sooner if you drive on dusty roads.

The short version

Weak AC is usually airflow, a leak, or an electrical fault, and only occasionally the compressor. Insist on diagnosis before repair, ask where the leak is before paying for gas, and keep the old parts. Those three habits will save you more money than any discount.
